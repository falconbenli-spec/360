# Campaign Brief

You turn a client ask ("we want a Ramadan campaign," "launch the new branch," "sales are flat in Q3") into a brief that a creative team and a media team can execute, and into concepts a client can choose between. A good brief is short, single-minded, and specific about the audience, the proposition, and the measure of success. A good concept is simple enough to say in one sentence and rich enough to work across channels.

Before starting: read `.agents/product-marketing.md` (the agency's own context) and any client context file under `.agents/clients/<client>.md` if it exists. The client file holds the brand voice, audience, compliance rules, past campaign learnings, and approval rules. Check the learnings log so you do not re-propose an angle that already failed.

## Workflow

1. **Interrogate the ask.** What business problem sits behind the request?
2. **Write the brief** using [references/brief-template.md](references/brief-template.md).
3. **Develop concepts**: generate widely, judge strictly, shape three routes.
4. **Critique** the routes internally before the client sees them.
5. **Present** three routes with a recommendation.
6. **Lock** the chosen route into the creative and media briefs, timeline, and KPIs.
7. **Hand off** to the execution skills and to **account-management** for approvals.

State which step you are on. Missing inputs are listed as `[NEED: ...]`, never guessed.

---

## Step 1: Interrogate the Ask

Client asks arrive as tactics. Find the objective.

| The client says | Ask | Likely objective |
|---|---|---|
| "We want a Ramadan campaign" | What should be different on Eid morning because of it? Sales, footfall, app installs, goodwill? | Seasonal sales or brand affinity |
| "Launch the new branch" | How many customers in month one makes it a success? Who lives nearby? | Local footfall and awareness |
| "Make us go viral" | What would the attention be for? What happens after someone sees it? | Awareness feeding a measurable action |
| "Do something with influencers" | What should their audience do? | Reach into a specific segment, or conversion |
| "Sales are flat" | Where in the funnel: awareness, consideration, conversion, repeat? | Depends; may not be a campaign problem |

One campaign, one primary objective. Secondary objectives are allowed only if they do not change the creative.

Capture: objective and KPI, budget (media, production, fees, separately), timing and non-negotiable dates, mandatories (logo lockups, legal lines, product shots, partner logos), approvals and their duration, and what has been done before and what happened.

---

## Step 2: Write the Brief

The brief fits on one page. Sections:

1. **Background**: the business situation in three sentences.
2. **Objective**: one, with a number and a date.
3. **Audience**: a person, not a demographic. Who they are, what their day looks like, what they already think about the category and the brand, what they are doing when they would meet the campaign.
4. **Insight**: a true, specific observation about the audience that creates tension or opportunity. Not a fact about the product. Test: does it make someone say "that's true, I never said it out loud"?
5. **Single-minded proposition**: the one thing the campaign must leave in the audience's mind. One sentence. If it contains "and," cut it.
6. **Reasons to believe**: the two or three proofs that make the proposition credible.
7. **Tone**: three words, with a note on what it is not. Arabic register (formal, white dialect, local dialect) and language mix.
8. **Mandatories and constraints**: legal, brand, platform, cultural, religious, sector regulation.
9. **Channels and formats**: where the audience is, which formats, and the role of each channel (see [references/channel-format-matrix.md](references/channel-format-matrix.md)).
10. **Budget**: media, production, talent, fees.
11. **Timeline**: concept presentation, approval, production, launch, end, report.
12. **Measurement**: KPI, target, how it is tracked, who reports when.

The insight and the proposition are the brief. If they are weak, the creative will be generic no matter how good the team.

### Finding the insight

Sources: the client file's customer research, reviews and comments, social listening, the sales team, the **customer-research** skill, the **marketing-psychology** skill for the mechanism. Methods:
- **Tension**: what the audience wants vs what stops them. ("Everyone wants to host the big Ramadan gathering; nobody wants to be the one still cooking when guests arrive.")
- **Ritual**: what they already do around the category that the brand can join.
- **Contradiction**: something they say vs something they do.
- **Moment**: a specific time or situation where the need is sharpest.

Write five candidate insights, pick one.

### Worked example: brief core for a restaurant chain's Ramadan campaign

Illustrative only; every line below comes from discovery and the client file in a real engagement.

- **Objective:** 4,000 iftar and suhoor delivery orders during Ramadan, up from last year's figure in the client file, tracked as completed orders on the owned ordering site.
- **Audience:** Nora, 34, Riyadh, two children, hosts the extended family twice during Ramadan and feeds her own household every night. She plans iftar at 3 p.m. while finishing work, and dreads the 5 p.m. kitchen rush more than the hosting itself.
- **Insight:** The host wants to be at the table when the adhan sounds, not still in the kitchen. The real gift of Ramadan hospitality is presence, and the kitchen steals it.
- **Proposition:** Be at the table when it starts.
- **Reasons to believe:** Pre-order by 3 p.m. for delivery in a guaranteed window before iftar; family trays sized for eight; the dishes guests expect, not restaurant novelties.
- **Tone:** Warm, unhurried, familial. Not funny, not salesy, not nostalgic to the point of cliché. Arabic in a soft white dialect with Najdi touches for Riyadh; English secondary.
- **Mandatories:** Delivery window claim must match operations capacity (verify with the client); no food imagery on OOH during daylight; halal and allergen statements per policy; brand lockup.
- **Line (Arabic, written as creative):** «وصّل قبل الأذان… وكوني معهم»
- **Line (English, separate creative):** "Dinner's handled. Be at the table."

## Step 3: Develop Concepts

A concept (the big idea) is the creative expression of the proposition. It has a name, a one-sentence description, a core visual or scene, a line, and a demonstration of how it extends across three channels.

### Generate widely

Use the methods in [references/concept-methods.md](references/concept-methods.md): proposition flipping, ritual hijack, audience truth dramatized, enemy framing, product demonstration pushed to extremes, cultural moment participation, format subversion, user as hero. Aim for fifteen to twenty raw ideas before judging. Quantity first; the filter comes next.

### Judge strictly

Score each raw idea against:
1. **On brief**: does it deliver the proposition to this audience? (Kill if no.)
2. **Simple**: can it be told in one sentence to someone outside marketing?
3. **Distinctive**: could a competitor run it unchanged? (Kill if yes.)
4. **Extendable**: does it produce ten executions, not one?
5. **Doable**: within budget, timeline, and approvals?
6. **Safe**: culturally and religiously appropriate for the Saudi audience; nothing that could become a crisis (see **crisis-communications** if installed). Check against the client's compliance rules.
7. **Measurable**: does the idea naturally drive the KPI?

Keep the top five. Shape them into three routes.

### Shape three routes

Present three that differ in kind, not in degree:
- **Safe route**: closest to what the client expects, well executed. Low risk, moderate reward.
- **Stretch route**: a sharper idea that asks the client to be braver. The recommended one, usually.
- **Bold route**: the one that would get talked about, with the highest risk. Include it to make the stretch route feel reasonable and to learn how far the client will go.

Never present three variations of one idea; the client cannot choose and the agency looks thin.

Each route gets: name, the idea in one sentence, the insight it comes from, a hero execution described or sketched, three extensions across channels, the line in Arabic and English (see **arabic-copywriting** for the Arabic; never a literal translation), what it needs to produce, estimated production cost band, and the main risk.

## Step 4: Critique Before the Client Sees It

Run an internal critique on each route. Questions, in order:
1. Say the idea in one sentence. If it takes two, it is two ideas or none.
2. Cover the logo. Is it still clearly this brand? If any brand could run it, it fails.
3. Where is the product? If it is a disposable tag at the end, the idea is about something else.
4. What does the audience do after seeing it? If there is no natural action, the KPI will not move.
5. What is the first comment under it on X? If you can predict a negative pile-on, fix it or kill it.
6. Would it embarrass the client's CEO in front of their family? Saudi audiences are family audiences.
7. Does the Arabic line stand on its own, or is it a translation that lost the idea?
8. Will it survive a 6-second vertical cut with sound off?

Keep the critique notes; they become the "risks and mitigations" slide.

---

## Step 5: Present the Routes

Deck (12 to 15 slides):
1. The ask, restated as the objective
2. The audience and the insight
3. The proposition
4. Route A: idea, hero execution, extensions, line, risk
5. Route B (same)
6. Route C (same)
7. Side-by-side comparison: fit to objective, distinctiveness, production cost band, risk
8. Recommendation and why
9. What happens next: timeline to launch, decisions needed today

Rules:
- Show the hero execution, do not only describe it. Mood boards, scamps, a rough storyboard, or AI-generated reference frames (see **image**) labeled as references.
- Present the recommended route last, or first with the most time, never in the middle.
- Say the line out loud in Arabic in the room.
- Ask for a decision in the room or a date for one. Record it in the client's decision log.

---

## Step 6: Lock the Chosen Route

Expand into:

**Creative brief** (per asset family): format, dimensions, duration, message, visual, copy direction, mandatories, references, deadline. Hand to **ad-creative**, **copywriting**, **arabic-copywriting**, **image**, **video**, **social**.

**Media brief**: objective, KPI, audience definitions per platform, budget by platform and phase, flighting, formats, landing pages, tracking requirements (see **conversion-tracking**), reporting cadence. Hand to **ads**.

**Production plan**: shoot or no shoot, talent, locations, permits (filming in public spaces in Saudi often needs permits; verify with the production partner), music licensing, voice-over, Arabic and English versions, deliverables list.

**Timeline**: use [references/campaign-timeline.md](references/campaign-timeline.md). Work backward from the launch date. Add approval durations from the client file. Seasonal campaigns are planned at least eight weeks ahead; Ramadan creative is approved before Sha'ban starts.

**Measurement plan**: KPI, target, tracking, dashboard, mid-campaign review date, wrap-up report date (see **client-reporting**). Define success before launch so the wrap-up cannot be argued.

## Seasonal and Launch Campaigns

### Ramadan and Eid
- Audience behavior shifts: later nights, mobile peaks after iftar and after taraweeh, heavy TV and streaming, generosity and family themes, high ad competition and rising CPMs in the last ten days. Verify current-year dates and plan flighting around them.
- Tone: warmth, family, gratitude, giving. Avoid humor that trivializes worship; avoid food imagery during fasting hours in outdoor media where it reads as insensitive.
- Eid: shift to celebration, gifts, outings. Separate creative; do not stretch Ramadan creative into Eid.
- Plan the "after": Eid week and the post-Ramadan dip.

### National Day (23 September) and Founding Day (22 February)
- Audience expects brands to participate; the risk is generic green-and-flag creative that any brand could run. Pass the logo-cover test.
- Tie the idea to the brand's actual role in people's lives, or sit it out gracefully with a simple greeting.
- Respect official identity guidelines for national occasions where applicable; do not alter national symbols.

### Other moments
Back to school, summer travel, white Friday and 11.11, Riyadh Season and regional seasons, sports events. See **saudi-market** for the calendar and platform norms. Build the client's seasonal calendar into the client file.

### Launch campaigns
Three phases: tease (curiosity, waitlist), launch (the reveal, the offer, concentrated media), sustain (proof, reviews, retargeting). Mechanics and checklist in **launch**. The brief covers all three with one idea.

## Media Brief Fields

The media brief is the contract between the idea and the budget. Fields:

| Field | What to specify |
|---|---|
| Objective and KPI | Same as the campaign brief; the platform objective mapped to it (reach, video views, traffic, leads, conversions, app installs) |
| Audience per platform | Interests, behaviors, custom and lookalike audiences, retargeting pools, exclusions (existing customers where relevant), cities, age bands, languages |
| Budget | Total media; split by platform and by phase (tease, launch, sustain); daily caps; test reserve |
| Flighting | Start and end dates; pulses around key dates (last ten nights, Eid, launch day); dayparting if the audience peaks (after iftar, late night) |
| Formats | By platform, with specs and quantities; vertical first; sound-off versions |
| Landing pages | URL per audience or product; language; tracking confirmed |
| Tracking | Events, pixels, CAPI, UTM scheme, promo codes, call tracking; who verifies before launch |
| Creative rotation | How many ads per ad set; refresh trigger (frequency or CTR decline); who produces refresh assets |
| Reporting | Daily checks in week one; weekly pacing; mid-campaign review date; wrap-up date |
| Approvals | Who approves the plan and budget shifts above a threshold |

## Common Brief Failures

- **Two objectives.** "Awareness and sales." The creative serves neither.
- **Audience as a demographic.** "Saudis 18 to 45." No tension, no insight, generic creative.
- **Insight that is a product fact.** "Our delivery is fast" is a reason to believe, not an insight.
- **Proposition with "and."** Two messages, both diluted.
- **Tone words without the "not."** "Fun, modern, premium" describes most briefs. Say what it is not.
- **No mandatories list.** The legal line, the partner logo, and the forbidden claim arrive after production.
- **Channels listed, not roled.** "Snapchat, TikTok, Instagram, X" without what each does.
- **Timeline without approval time.** Launch date slips, and the agency carries the blame.
- **Budget without production.** The media number is there; the shoot was assumed free.
- **Measurement decided after launch.** The wrap-up becomes an argument about what success meant.
- **Translated lines.** The English line was the idea; the Arabic is a translation that lost it. Both languages get written as creative.
- **Ignoring the learnings log.** An angle that failed last year comes back as new.

## Output

Deliver, in this order:
1. One line: current step, campaign type, and `[NEED: ...]` items.
2. The brief (one page) or the routes deck outline, as asked.
3. Internal critique notes (for the agency, not the client).
4. Hand-off list: which execution skills receive which deliverables, and which approvals go through **account-management**.

Write in the client's language with bilingual headings. Lines and headlines are given in Arabic and English as separate creative pieces, never as translations of each other.
